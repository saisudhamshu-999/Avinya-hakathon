Three steps, and none of them involve sending your report to a stranger

Upload happens in your browser. Nothing is transmitted to a server, stored in an account, or shared with an insurer or employer.

  1. Step one

    It reads the page

    Optical character recognition pulls the text out of your PDF or photo, then an extraction model separates each test into four parts: the name, the value, the unit and the reference range beside it.

  2. Step two

    It compares like with like

    Every value is checked against the range your own laboratory printed for it — not a generic table. Anything outside that range is marked, and anything the report left without a range is marked differently.

  3. Step three

    It writes it out in words

    Each test gets a plain explanation of what it measures and what commonly moves it. Results that are usually read together are grouped, and you get a one-page summary to hand to your doctor.

Where the line is

A tool that reads lab reports could easily drift into sounding like a doctor. Readout is built so it can't.

What Readout does

  • Names every test on the page and says what it measures
  • Marks values sitting outside the range your lab printed
  • Shows how far outside, and in which direction
  • Expands abbreviations like SGPT, PCV and 25(OH)D
  • Points out results usually read as a group
  • Drafts questions worth asking at your appointment
  • Builds a one-page summary you can print or email

What it will never do

  • Name a condition, or suggest you might have one
  • Tell you a result is nothing to worry about
  • Recommend a medicine, dose or supplement
  • Tell you whether to go to hospital, or when
  • Predict what will happen next
  • Keep a copy of your report or send it anywhere
  • Stand in for an appointment you were going to make

Built around one honest question: what do I ask the doctor?

Plain language

Every test, expanded

Tap any result to read what the test measures, what commonly moves it up or down, and what the abbreviation on your report stands for. Medical terms stay available, but they are never the first thing you meet.

Patterns

Results that travel together

Some tests are only meaningful next to others — glucose beside HbA1c, creatinine beside eGFR. Readout groups those and explains the relationship, without drawing a conclusion from it.

Hand-off

A page for your doctor

Add why the test was ordered and what you want to know, and Readout assembles it with the flagged values into a single printable page. You add the context; it only does the typing.

Questions people ask first

Does a flag mean something is wrong?

No. A flag means only that the number printed on your report sits outside the range printed next to it. Plenty of healthy people have results outside a reference range, and plenty of ranges differ between laboratories for the same test. A flag is a prompt to ask, not an answer.

Where does my report go?

Nowhere. Text recognition and explanation run inside your browser, so the file never leaves the device. If you turn on history, past results are kept in your browser's local storage on this device only, and you can erase them from Settings at any time.

My report is a photo taken on a phone. Will that work?

Usually. Flat, evenly lit, in focus, with the whole page in frame gives the best extraction. Readout shows you every value it read so you can check them against the paper before relying on anything.

What if a result looks alarming?

Contact a clinician rather than reading further here. Readout deliberately does not rank results by severity or tell you how urgent anything is, because that judgement depends on your history, your symptoms and your medicines — none of which are on the page it read.

Can I use it for someone else's report?

Yes, with their agreement — parents and carers often do. The summary page has a field for who the report belongs to so the printed page is not ambiguous at the appointment.

Bring the paper. Bring the questions too.

Readout takes about twenty seconds on a typical three-page panel, and gives you something to say when the appointment starts.
